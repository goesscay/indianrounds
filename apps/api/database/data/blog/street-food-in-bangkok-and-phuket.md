Thai street food is cheap, quick and often excellent, and Bangkok and Phuket are two of the easiest places to enjoy it. Head for busy stalls, order a few small dishes and keep an open mind about spice. Vegetarian, halal and mild options exist, though you will need to ask clearly.

## Why Bangkok is a street-food city

Bangkok runs on street food. Locals eat out for most meals because it is quick and affordable, so there are carts, shophouses and open-air stalls on almost every road. Food tends to be cooked in front of you, which makes it easy to see what you are getting.

![Grand Palace Bangkok](/photos/places/thailand/grand-palace-bangkok.jpg)

Some areas worth a visit:

- **Yaowarat (Chinatown):** Comes alive after dusk, with seafood grills, noodle shops, roast duck, dumplings, desserts and sweet soups lining the road. It is crowded, noisy and delicious.
- **Night markets:** Large markets such as the Chatuchak weekend market (for daytime snacks) and the evening markets in areas like Rot Fai and Asiatique offer snacks, drinks and mixed seating.
- **Sukhumvit side streets:** Everyday eateries and food courts in shopping malls serve street-style food in air-conditioned comfort, which is a gentle introduction for anyone worried about hygiene.
- **Old Town (Rattanakosin):** Simple noodle and rice shops near the Grand Palace and temples, handy for lunch between sights.

## Dishes to try

You will see a long list of dishes, but a handful give you a good first taste.

1. **Pad thai:** Stir-fried rice noodles with egg, tofu or prawns, peanuts and lime. A safe and tasty start.
2. **Som tam:** A spicy green papaya salad, pounded in a mortar. Ask for less chilli, as it is usually fiery.
3. **Khao pad:** Thai fried rice, simple and mild with egg and your choice of protein.
4. **Pad kra pao:** Stir-fried basil with chicken, pork or tofu, often served with a fried egg over rice.
5. **Tom yum and tom kha:** Hot and sour soup, or the milder coconut-based version.
6. **Khao soi:** A curry noodle soup from northern Thailand, found at many stalls.
7. **Satay and grilled skewers:** Served with peanut sauce.
8. **Mango sticky rice:** The classic dessert, best when mangoes are in season (roughly spring to early summer).
9. **Roti with banana and condensed milk:** A crispy sweet crepe, popular with travellers.
10. **Fresh fruit and coconut ice cream:** Perfect after a spicy meal.

## Phuket's street-food scene

Phuket is known for beaches, but its Old Town is a good place to eat. The streets of Phuket Town have Sino-Portuguese shophouses, and the local food reflects southern Thai and Chinese influences. Look for:

![Big Buddha Phuket](/photos/places/thailand/big-buddha-phuket.jpg)

- **Hokkien-style noodles** and dim sum-style small plates.
- **Southern curries** that are stronger and spicier than central Thai food.
- **Fresh seafood** at seaside stalls and markets, usually grilled or fried.
- **Weekend night markets** in Phuket Town, with snacks and local sweets.
- **Muslim-run stalls** serving roti, curries and satay, common in the south.

Beach areas such as Patong have plenty of eateries, but street food there is geared to tourists, so prices are higher and quality varies. Go a little away from the main strip for more local meals.

> **Tip:** Learn two phrases. "Mai phet" means not spicy, and "phet nit noi" means a little spicy. Fish sauce and shrimp paste are very common, so vegetarians should ask clearly, or show a written note in Thai.

## Vegetarian, Jain and halal choices

Vegetarian food exists in Thailand, but fish sauce, oyster sauce and shrimp paste appear in many dishes, so a vegetable stir-fry is not always vegetarian. During the Vegetarian Festival in Phuket, usually in autumn, many stalls display yellow flags for "jay" food, which excludes meat, and also avoids strong-smelling vegetables such as garlic. Dates change every year, so check ahead.

Jain travellers will find it hard to eat out and may want to plan ahead. Halal options are easier to find in Muslim-run areas and in Phuket, and there are Indian restaurants in both cities that serve familiar food if you need a break.

## Staying stomach-safe

Street food is generally fine if you use common sense:

- Choose busy stalls with high turnover and freshly cooked food.
- Avoid anything that has been sitting out.
- Drink bottled water, and be cautious about ice in very basic places.
- Start mild, and add chilli gradually.
- Carry basic stomach medicines and rehydration salts.

## How much to budget

Street meals cost far less than restaurant meals. A plate of noodles or rice is cheap, a dessert is cheaper, and a sit-down seafood dinner is where bills climb. Prices change, and tourist areas charge more, so check the menu before ordering. Cash in baht is most useful at stalls, though QR payments are increasingly common.
