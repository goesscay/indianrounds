The classic Uzbekistan route runs Tashkent, Samarkand, Bukhara and Khiva, and it works best over seven to nine nights. Samarkand is the grandest, Bukhara the most atmospheric and Khiva the most complete, so each deserves at least a night. Fast trains connect the first three, which makes the country easier to travel than many first-timers expect.

## Why the Silk Road cities matter

For centuries, caravans carrying silk, spices and ideas passed through these oasis cities. What remains today are blue-tiled domes, covered bazaars, madrassas and old trading domes that feel nothing like a typical city break.

Many travellers from India enjoy the shared history. Mughal ruler Babur came from this region, and you will find echoes of that heritage in the architecture. Rules for visas and entry change, so check the current requirements for Indian passport holders before you book.

## Tashkent: the starting point

Most trips begin with a night or two in the capital. The **old town** around the Hazrati Imam complex has a historic library, mosques and madrassas. The **Tashkent Metro**, with its decorated stations, is an inexpensive way to see something unusual; check current photography rules first.

![Hazrati Imam Tashkent](/photos/places/uzbekistan/hazrati-imam-tashkent.jpg)

You can also visit **Chorsu Bazaar** for dried fruit, spices, breads and sweets under a large domed roof. It is a good place to try your first plov.

## The cities: Samarkand, Bukhara and Khiva

### Samarkand: the grand one

Plan two nights. The **Registan**, three monumental madrassas facing a central square, is the symbol of the country and is best seen in late afternoon and again after dark when it is lit.

![Itchan Kala Khiva](/photos/places/uzbekistan/itchan-kala-khiva.jpg)

Nearby, **Gur-e-Amir** is the tomb of Timur, with a gold-and-blue interior. **Shah-i-Zinda** is a street of tombs with some of the finest tile work in Central Asia, usually the favourite stop for photographers. The **Bibi-Khanym Mosque** and the **Siab Bazaar** complete a full day.

> **Tip:** Visit the Registan at sunrise or after sunset. Midday light is harsh and tour groups arrive in the late morning.

### Bukhara: the best for wandering

Bukhara deserves two nights, and a slow pace. Its old centre is compact and walkable, with domed bazaars, courtyard cafes and madrassas converted into craft shops. The **Kalyan Minaret**, a tall brick tower, anchors the old city. The **Ark Fortress** and the **Lyabi-Hauz** pool, shaded by old mulberry trees, are good for an evening stroll.

Look for suzani embroidery, ceramics, knives and silk scarves in the old trading domes. Prices are often negotiable, so be friendly and unhurried.

### Khiva: a walled town

Khiva's **Itchan Kala** is an entire walled inner town preserved much as it was, with mud-brick walls, minarets and carved wooden pillars. Stay one night inside or near the walls so you can see it in the early and late light, when the day visitors leave.

Climb a minaret for views over the rooftops, and walk the walls at sunset. Khiva is a long way from the others, so build in time for the journey.

## How to link the cities

A sensible order is Tashkent, Samarkand, Bukhara, Khiva and back, but timings and routes change, so confirm before you plan.

1. **Tashkent to Samarkand:** a fast train usually takes a few hours.
2. **Samarkand to Bukhara:** train or road, again a few hours.
3. **Bukhara to Khiva:** a long road journey across desert, or a short domestic flight.
4. **Khiva back to Tashkent:** typically a domestic flight.

Check current train and flight schedules close to your travel dates. We usually suggest booking tickets early for popular times.

## Best time to go

Spring and autumn are the most comfortable, with warm days and pleasant evenings. Summer can be very hot, especially in Bukhara and Khiva, so go early in the day and rest in the afternoon. Winter is cold and quiet, and some sites have shorter hours.

## Food and everyday details

**Plov**, the national dish, is rice cooked with carrots and meat. Vegetarians can ask for versions without meat, though not every kitchen will have one. **Samsa** (baked pastries), fresh bread, melons, dried fruit and green tea are all easy, and bread is stamped and baked in clay ovens in front of you.

Alcohol is available in most cities, but the culture is relaxed rather than lively at night. Dress modestly at religious sites, and carry a scarf or shawl.

Carry small notes for tips and bazaar purchases, and check current rules about changing and carrying currency before you travel.

## Is it right for you?

Uzbekistan suits travellers who love architecture, markets and slow wandering more than adventure or nightlife. It is also friendly for older family members, as walking distances are moderate and the pace is gentle.
